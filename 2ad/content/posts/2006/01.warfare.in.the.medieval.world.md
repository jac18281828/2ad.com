This book follows more than a thousand years of war. It begins with Justinian's Byzantium and
ends with the Thirty Years' War. Carey's combined-arms system follows the same four weapon
systems as heavy cavalry rises, rules the field and gives way to infantry and gunpowder. It
picks up where [Volume 1]({filename}/posts/2005/01.warfare.in.the.ancient.world.md) left off.

### Manzikert, 1071 CE

By the late 1060s the Seljuk Turks were moving into Byzantine Anatolia. Their horse archers wore
little armour and fought with the short composite bow, striking from a distance and retreating
when charged, sometimes for days. Byzantine doctrine answered them with light infantry bowmen
beside the cavalry, flanks covered and the army kept together.

In the summer of 1071 the emperor Romanus IV Diogenes gathered perhaps 30,000 men at Erzurum to
retake Manzikert. The sultan Alp Arslan, camped at Aleppo, turned his army around and marched
for the frontier. Romanus had sent his light infantry to a siege elsewhere, breaking what the
book calls "the central canon of warfare against steppe light cavalry" (*Warfare in the
Medieval World*, pp. 64–66).

On 19 August Romanus advanced with his heavy cavalry in a single broad line. The Turkish centre
fell back before him while the wings showered his flanks with arrows. As evening came, he
ordered the army back to camp, and his second line, under Andronicus Ducas, rode off and left
the emperor to the Turks.

> **Manzikert shows what heavy cavalry cannot do alone.**
