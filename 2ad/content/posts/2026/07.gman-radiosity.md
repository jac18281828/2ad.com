## A wall is a large, dim lamp

Every surface is a light source. A matte surface reflects part of the light that reaches it and scatters it evenly in every direction. A red wall lights the sphere beside it red.

How much does the bouncing add? Put a lamp in a closed room painted white. White paint reflects about 80 percent of the light that reaches it. The lamp's light hits a wall and 80 percent of it leaves again. That light hits another wall and 80 percent of it leaves again, and so on. The walls end up with the direct light times

```
1 + 0.8 + 0.8² + 0.8³ + ⋯ = 1 / (1 − 0.8) = 5
```

Four fifths of what the walls receive has bounced at least once. For walls of reflectance ρ the sum is 1 / (1 − ρ), and the bounced share is ρ itself.

## Where the light lands

Take one small patch of wall. Of the light it sends out, some fraction lands on a given patch across the room. That fraction is the form factor, and three things set it:

- **Distance.** A patch twice as far away looks a quarter the size and catches a quarter of the light.
- **Tilt, at both ends.** A surface sends the most light straight out from its face and none along it, and a patch catches the most face-on and none edge-on.
- **Whatever is in the way.** A blocked patch catches nothing.

The light has to land somewhere, so in a closed room a patch's form factors add up to 1. Brightness and colour play no part; the form factors are geometry alone. For a small patch of area A at distance r, with each end tilted by its angle θ from facing the other:

```
F = cos θ_send × cos θ_receive × A / (π r²)
```

The π makes the fractions add up to 1.

Inside a sphere, distance and tilt cancel. The chord between two points on a sphere of radius R meets the wall at the same angle θ at both ends, and its length is r = 2R cos θ. The form factor becomes cos² θ × A / (π × 4R² cos² θ) = A / (4πR²): the patch's share of the sphere's area, wherever it sits. Light a closed sphere from its centre and every point on the wall receives the same bounce light, ρ / (1 − ρ) times the direct light: the bounced part of the white room's sum. gman's radiosity solver and its path tracer are both tested against that sphere.

Written for every patch at once, the bounces and the form factors make one equation. Patch i sends out the light it makes plus ρ of the light that lands on it:

```
P_i = E_i + ρ_i × Σ_j F_ji × P_j
```

F_ji is the fraction of patch j's light that lands on patch i. The light a patch sends out per unit area, P_i divided by its area, is its radiosity, the quantity the method is named for. Substitute the equation into itself and each substitution adds one bounce: the series 1 + ρ + ρ² + ⋯ again, with the geometry carried along.

A matte patch looks equally bright from every direction, so one number describes it and the solution holds for every viewpoint. A scene solved once renders from any camera.

## From heat to the Cornell box

The radiosity equation began in heat transfer, where engineers use it for radiant heat exchanged between surfaces and call F the view factor. In 1984 Goral, Torrance, Greenberg and Battaile, at Cornell, applied it to light and rendered a box with coloured walls. The box above descends from theirs, and graphics researchers have tested renderers against a Cornell box ever since.

A direct solve needs n² form factors: every patch sees every other, so 10,000 patches need 100 million. In 1988 Cohen, Chen, Wallace and Greenberg replaced the direct solve with progressive refinement. Each step picks the patch holding the most light it has yet to pass on and shoots that light to every other patch. The largest contributions arrive first, so the image is usable after a few steps and improves with each one.

Radiosity handles diffuse surfaces only. A mirror's brightness depends on the viewing direction, which one number per patch cannot represent. Wallace, Cohen and Greenberg (1987) combined the two methods: radiosity for light scattered between diffuse surfaces, ray tracing for reflection and refraction.

## How gman solves it

gman's radiosity pass follows the 1987 two-pass design and solves by progressive refinement inside the ray tracer:

- The ray tracer computes direct light, so each patch starts with ρ times its direct light as unshot light, and the light sources stay out of the solve.
- Each step shoots from the patch with the most unshot power, its unshot light times its area.
- Shadow rays give the form factors, by the disc approximation of Wallace, Elmquist and Haines (1989). Each shooting patch is sampled at 16 points, and a ray from the receiving patch to each point tests visibility. A translucent surface in the way transmits a share 1 − Os.
- The solve stops when the unshot power falls to a thousandth of the direct power.
- At render time, `ambient()` adds each point's bounce light, scaled by the surface's `Ka`.

On an Apple M3 Max the box renders in 0.5 seconds with direct light only and in 1.8 seconds with the pass.

## Try it

Install gman with the tarball's `install.sh`, as the [README](https://github.com/jac18281828/gman#install) shows. Then download [`box.rib`](https://gist.github.com/jac18281828/9ee7d3e371d254340d00dee9045eacda) and render it:

```sh
gman -r gmanraytracer box.rib
```

That writes `box.png`, the right-hand render. For the left, delete the `Option` line and set `Ka` to 0. `Option "radiosity" "float elementsize"` sets the patch size, by default an eighth of the scene's largest side. Smaller patches resolve finer shadows and take longer.

## Checking against the path tracer

![the same box path-traced: the shadows fill and the spheres take the walls' colours as in the radiosity render, with visible grain]({static}/images/2026/gman-cornell-path.png)

Kajiya's rendering equation (1986) covers every kind of surface; radiosity solves its diffuse case on a mesh. A path tracer estimates the full equation by tracing random light paths from the camera, so it checks the pass independently. Replace the `Option` line with `Option "pathtracer" "integer samples" [64]`, set `Ka` to 0 and run `gman -r gmanpathtracer box.rib`. At 64 paths per subpixel, the red side of the left sphere is (94, 33, 31), against (99, 39, 37) from the pass.

The path tracer took 76 seconds and its image is still grainy; the pass took 1.8 seconds and its image is smooth. The pass's cost is resolution: it solves light per patch and smooths between patches, so detail smaller than a patch blurs.
