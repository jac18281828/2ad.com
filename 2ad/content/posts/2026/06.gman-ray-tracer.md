One equation describes all the light in a scene:

```
L = L_e + K*L
```

L is the light travelling in every direction from every point, L_e the light the sources emit and K the operator that carries light one bounce. Expanded, it is `L_e + K*L_e + K²*L_e + ⋯`: light straight from a source, plus light that has bounced once, plus light that has bounced twice, and so on. A pixel's colour is the sum of those terms along the light that reaches it. A ray tracer computes the sum one bounce at a time, and each bounce is one more ray.

The two renders above are one scene. gman's z-buffer, its fast preview, shades each surface from its lights alone and never asks what stands between them, so nothing casts a shadow. The ray tracer asks, and the table, the robot and the flowers throw shadows across the floor and up the wall.

## I'm not backwards, the light is

A ray tracer follows light from the eye to the lamp, the reverse of the way it travels. Of the light leaving a lamp, almost none reaches your eye, and a light path carries the same light run in either direction, so the renderer starts where the answer is needed: one ray from the eye through each pixel. The vase above is 640 by 400 pixels at 4 samples each, 1,024,000 rays from the eye.

A ray is a starting point O and a direction D, and every point along it is O + tD for some distance t. A sphere of centre C and radius R is every point P with |P − C|² = R². Put the ray into the sphere:

```
(D·D) t² + 2 D·(O − C) t + |O − C|² − R² = 0
```

The equation is a quadratic in t. A negative discriminant means the ray misses. Otherwise the two roots are where the ray enters and leaves the sphere, and the smaller positive one is the hit. Every primitive gman draws answers the same question, how far along this ray is my surface, and the ray tracer keeps the nearest answer.

## One term per bounce

At the nearest hit, the ray tracer fires one more ray toward each light. A light the ray reaches adds its share, and a light behind something opaque adds nothing. That is `K*L_e`, direct light, and those rays make the shadows.

A mirror adds the next term by firing one ray more. gman's `mirror` shader is C++, but in RenderMan's shading language it is one line:

```
Ci = Os * Cs * Kr * trace(P, R);
```

`R` is the reflected direction and `trace()` fires a ray along it. Whatever that ray finds, the mirror returns scaled by `Kr`. If it finds another mirror, that one fires another ray: each call is one more application of K, one more term of the sum.

![the robot's dome as a mirror, showing the room]({static}/images/2026/gman-mirror.png)

Glass fires two. Part of the light reflects and the rest bends through the surface by Snell's law, sin θ₁ = n sin θ₂, with n = 1.5 for glass. The Fresnel equations set the split: face-on, glass reflects ((n − 1) / (n + 1))² = 4 percent, and at a grazing angle it reflects nearly everything, which is why the edges of a glass vase look like mirrors. Inside the glass, light meeting the surface more than 41.8° from face-on cannot get out at all and reflects entirely.

![the vase in glass, the room bent through it]({static}/images/2026/gman-glass.png)

Each ray can spawn more, so a pixel grows a tree of rays. gman stops a branch after four bounces and gives it the background colour. Turner Whitted described this recursive ray tracer in 1980.

## The terms it skips

A Whitted ray tracer follows light only where the direction is certain: toward a light, off a mirror and through glass. The light a matte wall passes to another matte wall scatters in every direction, and following it would take a fan of rays at every hit. The ray tracer drops those terms. The vase scene stands in for them with `LightSource "ambientlight" 1 "intensity" [0.38]`, one constant brightness added everywhere, a guess.

Radiosity computes the terms the ray tracer drops, and it is the next article in this series.

## Try it

Install gman with the tarball's `install.sh`, as the [README](https://github.com/jac18281828/gman#install) shows. Then, from the unpacked folder:

```sh
gman -r gmanraytracer samples/vase.rib
```

That writes `vase.png`, the ray-traced render above, in about 2.4 seconds on an Apple M3 Max. Plain `gman samples/vase.rib` renders the z-buffer preview. The [gman page](/gman.html) shows the one-line edits that turn the vase to glass and the dome to a mirror.
