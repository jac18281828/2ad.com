A path tracer follows light along random paths through a scene, bouncing off
every surface it meets, and averages what the paths carry back to the eye.

[What Is Ray Tracing?]({filename}06.gman-ray-tracer.md) followed light off
mirrors and through glass and faked the rest with a constant `ambientlight`.
[What Is Radiosity?]({filename}07.gman-radiosity.md) computed the light passed
between matte walls, and only matte ones, because a patch stores one number. A
path tracer handles mirror, glass and matte alike, since each bounce draws its
next direction from whatever surface it lands on.

| Ray tracer | Path tracer |
|---|---|
| ![a room in which a tilted table drops a vase as a small robot braces against the table's edge, ray-traced: the shadows filled by a constant ambient light]({static}/images/2026/gman-vase-raytraced.png) | ![the same room path-traced: the light in the shadows and on the robot comes from bounces off the walls, floor and table]({static}/images/2026/gman-vase-pathtraced.png) |

The left render fills its shadows with a constant ambient light. On the right,
the path tracer fills them with light bounced off the walls, floor and table,
and it skips the scene's `ambientlight` and says so:

```
gmanpathtracer: ambientlight lights nothing under the path tracer; skipped 1 light(s).
```

## Every path is insufficient

A single random path gives a poor estimate of a pixel, and the average of many
gives the right one. Monte Carlo estimates a sum by averaging random samples of
it. Stand at one point on the ceiling of the box and ask how much light reaches
it. The answer is the light arriving from every direction below the ceiling,
each weighted by how squarely it strikes. Look in one direction chosen at random
and you see the red wall, the floor or the top of the shade, and one look says
little about the total. Average a hundred looks and the estimate settles. The
error falls as one over the square root of the count, so four times the looks
halves it. The looks have to be fair. A tracer that looks toward the bright
floor more often counts each of those looks for less, in proportion to how much
more often it chose that direction, so the average stays true.

The white room from the radiosity article shows how that applies to light. Its
walls reflect 80 percent, and its light is the sum

<div class="highlight"><pre><code>1 + 0.8 + 0.8² + 0.8³ + ⋯ = 5</code></pre></div>

Monte Carlo finds the 5 without the formula. Send out a path. At each wall it
survives with probability 0.8 and ends otherwise; count the walls it hits. One
path hits 1 wall and ends, and another hits 9. A path reaches its k-th wall with
probability 0.8<sup>k−1</sup>, so the average count over many paths is exactly
the sum, 5.

The coin flip at each wall is Russian roulette, and it is how a path tracer ends
paths without dropping light. A fixed cap drops terms, and stopping the white
room after four bounces loses 0.8⁴, 41 percent of the light. The ray tracer caps
its mirror and glass chains at four bounces, which few scenes reach. A path
tracer follows every matte bounce, where a cap costs real light. Roulette keeps
every term on average. A path survives with probability q and, when it
survives, carries its light divided by q, so the expected light is unchanged.
The path tracer plays roulette from a path's fourth vertex, with q the share of
light the path still carries, capped at 0.95.

## A path, one bounce at a time

A path starts at the eye, one ray through the pixel, as in the ray tracer. At
each surface it hits, it does three things:

- **It asks one light.** A shadow ray toward one light, chosen in proportion to
  how much it contributes, adds that light's direct term. This is next-event
  estimation, the ray tracer's shadow ray cast at every bounce.
- **It picks a direction at random.** The surface's BSDF, the rule for how the
  material scatters light, draws the next direction. A matte surface scatters
  anywhere over the hemisphere, most often near the normal; a glossy one near
  the mirror direction; a mirror and glass exactly.
- **It keeps what the surface keeps.** The path's throughput shrinks by the
  surface's reflectance, and roulette decides whether it goes on.

The pixel's colour is the average of the light its paths carry back. A random
bounce sometimes lands on a lamp the shadow ray could also have picked.
Multiple importance sampling, from Veach's 1997 thesis, blends the two
estimates, so a small bright lamp or a sharp glossy highlight converges without
fireflies, the stray white pixels a lucky path leaves behind.

Lamps can be shapes. `AreaLightSource "arealight"` turns the `Sphere` or `Disk`
after it into a light, and a lamp with size casts a soft shadow.
[`samples/materials.rib`](https://github.com/jac18281828/gman/blob/main/samples/materials.rib)
lights four materials with two lamps, a warm disk overhead and out of frame and
the small blue sphere at the left. An area light shines from its outward face
only, and the first version of this scene lit the ceiling.

![gman's path-traced render of samples/materials.rib: from the left, a gold ball, a glass ball, a mirror ball taking the clay wall's colour and a blue plastic torus, each pooling a soft shadow on the floor, with a small bright sphere lamp at the upper left]({static}/images/2026/gman-materials-pathtraced.png)

From the left are gold `shinymetal`, `glass`, a `mirror` and a `plastic` torus.
The picture traces 256 paths a subpixel. The sample traces 64, about two
minutes on one core of an Apple M3 Max:

```sh
gman -r gmanpathtracer samples/materials.rib
```

## Two answers to one equation

| Radiosity | Path tracer |
|---|---|
| ![the Cornell box from the radiosity article, rendered by the radiosity pass: smooth, with the ceiling lit by bounce light and the bulb glowing under its shade]({static}/images/2026/gman-box-radiosity.png) | ![the same box path-traced: the same colours with visible grain, and the bulb and its shade dark]({static}/images/2026/gman-box-path.png) |

Both renders are the box from the radiosity article, and they agree on the
light. The side of the left sphere facing the red wall measures (104, 50, 44)
from the radiosity pass and (104, 49, 43) from the path tracer. The ceiling, lit
by bounce alone, measures (98, 89, 74) against (99, 89, 74). They part in the
shadow under the left sphere, (61, 48, 40) against (49, 36, 30), where a patch
averages the light across its width and lifts the dark. At 64 paths per subpixel
the path tracer took four minutes on an Apple M3 Max and still shows grain; the
radiosity pass took 17 seconds and shows none.

On the right the lamp hangs dark. Its bulb, shade and cord show by
`ambientlight`s, and the path tracer skips all three, so the spotlight under the
shade lights the room from a lamp that looks switched off.

Grain is how Monte Carlo reports its error, and quadrupling the paths halves it.
The radiosity pass trades grain for blur. Its error is the patch size, and only
smaller patches shrink it. The path tracer draws its random numbers from a fixed
seed, so a scene renders the same picture every time, grain included.

To render it, replace the two `Option` lines in
[`box.rib`](https://gist.github.com/jac18281828/9ee7d3e371d254340d00dee9045eacda)
with `Option "pathtracer" "integer samples" [64]`, set the first `Ka` to 0 and
run `gman -r gmanpathtracer box.rib`.

## Four ways to solve it

Radiosity and path tracing differ in two choices, where the light is stored and
how the equation is solved. The other two combinations exist too:

| | Deterministic | Random |
|---|---|---|
| **Patches** | radiosity | stochastic radiosity |
| **Points** | ray tracing | path tracing |

Radiosity stores light on patches and solves exactly. Stochastic radiosity keeps
the patches and shoots light at random. A Whitted ray tracer works at points and
follows each ray exactly, along mirrors and glass only. A path tracer works at
points and samples every bounce at random. All four answer
L = L<sub>e</sub> + K\*L, and production renderers mix them, caching the
diffuse light a solver computes and path tracing the rest.

## Try it

Install gman with the tarball's `install.sh`, as the
[README](https://github.com/jac18281828/gman#install) shows. Then add one line
to `samples/vase.rib` in the unpacked folder, under `PixelSamples 2 2`:

```
Option "pathtracer" "integer samples" [64]
```

and render it through the path tracer:

```sh
gman -r gmanpathtracer samples/vase.rib
```

That writes the path-traced vase above, in about three minutes on one core of
an Apple M3 Max, since the path tracer renders on one thread.
