[What Is Ray Tracing?]({filename}06.gman-ray-tracer.md) followed light off mirrors and through glass and faked the rest with a constant `ambientlight`. [What Is Radiosity?]({filename}07.gman-radiosity.md) computed the light passed between matte surfaces with a solver on patches. A path tracer computes every term of the rendering equation, mirror, glass and matte alike, by Monte Carlo: it follows random paths of light and averages them. gman's path tracer has no use for the vase scene's `ambientlight` and says so:

```
gmanpathtracer: ambientlight lights nothing under the path tracer; skipped 1 light(s).
```

## Every path is wrong

A single random path gives a poor estimate of a pixel, and the average of many gives the right one. That is Monte Carlo: estimate a sum by averaging random samples of it. To find the average height of a crowd, measure a hundred people chosen at random. No one of them is the average, but the mean of the hundred is close, and its error falls as one over the square root of the count: four times the people, half the error.

The white room from the radiosity article shows how that applies to light. Its walls reflect 80 percent, and its light is the sum

<div class="highlight"><pre><code>1 + 0.8 + 0.8² + 0.8³ + ⋯ = 5</code></pre></div>

Monte Carlo finds the 5 without the formula. Send out a path. At each wall it survives with probability 0.8 and ends otherwise; count the walls it hits. One path hits 1 wall and ends; another hits 9. A path reaches its k-th wall with probability 0.8<sup>k−1</sup>, so the average count over many paths is exactly the sum: 5.

The coin flip at each wall is Russian roulette, and it is how a path tracer ends paths without dropping light. A fixed cap drops terms: stopping the white room after four bounces loses 0.8⁴, 41 percent of the light. The ray tracer caps its mirror and glass chains at four bounces, which few scenes reach; a path tracer follows every matte bounce, where a cap costs real light. Roulette keeps every term on average. A path survives with probability q and, when it survives, carries its light divided by q, so the expected light is unchanged. gman plays roulette from a path's fourth vertex, with q the share of light the path still carries, capped at 0.95.

## A path, one bounce at a time

A path starts at the eye, one ray through the pixel, as in the ray tracer. At each surface it hits, it does three things:

- **It asks one light.** A shadow ray toward one light, chosen in proportion to how much it contributes, adds that light's direct term. This is next-event estimation: the ray tracer's shadow ray, now at every bounce.
- **It picks a direction at random.** The surface's BSDF, the rule for how the material scatters light, draws the next direction. A matte surface scatters anywhere over the hemisphere, most often near the normal; a glossy one near the mirror direction; a mirror and glass exactly.
- **It keeps what the surface keeps.** The path's throughput shrinks by the surface's reflectance, and roulette decides whether it goes on.

The pixel's colour is the average of the light its paths carry back. A random bounce sometimes lands on a lamp the shadow ray could also have picked. Multiple importance sampling, from Veach's 1997 thesis, blends the two estimates, so a small bright lamp or a sharp glossy highlight converges without fireflies.

Lamps can be shapes. `AreaLightSource "arealight"` turns the `Sphere` or `Disk` after it into a light, and a lamp with size casts a soft shadow. gman's [`samples/materials.rib`](https://github.com/jac18281828/gman/blob/main/samples/materials.rib) lights four materials with two lamps: a warm disk overhead, out of frame, and the small blue sphere at the left. An area light shines from its outward face only, and the first version of this scene lit the ceiling.

![gman's path-traced render of samples/materials.rib: from the left, a gold ball, a glass ball, a mirror ball taking the clay wall's colour and a blue plastic torus, each pooling a soft shadow on the floor, with a small bright sphere lamp at the upper left]({static}/images/2026/gman-materials-pathtraced.png)

From the left: gold `shinymetal`, `glass`, a `mirror` and a `plastic` torus. The picture traces 256 paths a subpixel. The sample traces 64, about two minutes on one core of an Apple M3 Max:

```sh
gman -r gmanpathtracer samples/materials.rib
```

## Two answers to one equation

| Radiosity | Path tracer |
|---|---|
| ![the Cornell box from the radiosity article, rendered by the radiosity pass: smooth, the spheres red and green on the sides facing the walls]({static}/images/2026/gman-cornell-radiosity.png) | ![the same box path-traced: the same colours, with visible grain]({static}/images/2026/gman-cornell-path.png) |

The box from the radiosity article, rendered both ways. They agree: the side of the left sphere facing the red wall is (99, 39, 37) from the radiosity pass and (94, 33, 31) from the path tracer. At 64 paths per subpixel the path tracer took 76 seconds on an Apple M3 Max and still shows grain; the radiosity pass took 1.8 seconds and shows none.

Grain is how Monte Carlo reports its error, and quadrupling the paths halves it. The radiosity pass has no grain, and its error is the patch size, which no amount of solving shrinks. gman draws its random numbers from a fixed seed, so a scene renders the same picture every time, grain included.

To render it, replace the `Option` line in [`box.rib`](https://gist.github.com/jac18281828/9ee7d3e371d254340d00dee9045eacda) with `Option "pathtracer" "integer samples" [64]`, set `Ka` to 0 and run `gman -r gmanpathtracer box.rib`.

## Four ways to solve it

Radiosity and path tracing differ in two choices: where the light is stored, and how the equation is solved. The other two combinations exist too:

| | Deterministic | Random |
|---|---|---|
| **Patches** | radiosity | stochastic radiosity |
| **Points** | ray tracing | path tracing |

Radiosity stores light on patches and solves exactly. Stochastic radiosity keeps the patches and shoots light at random. A Whitted ray tracer works at points and follows each ray exactly, along mirrors and glass only. A path tracer works at points and samples every bounce at random. All four answer L = L<sub>e</sub> + K\*L, and production renderers mix them, caching the diffuse light a solver computes and path tracing the rest.

## Try it

Install gman with the tarball's `install.sh`, as the [README](https://github.com/jac18281828/gman#install) shows. Then add one line to `samples/vase.rib` in the unpacked folder, under `PixelSamples 2 2`:

```
Option "pathtracer" "integer samples" [64]
```

and render it through the path tracer:

```sh
gman -r gmanpathtracer samples/vase.rib
```

That writes the path-traced vase above, in about three minutes on one core of an Apple M3 Max; the path tracer renders on one thread.
